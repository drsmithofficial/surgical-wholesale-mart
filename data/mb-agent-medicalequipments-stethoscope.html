Frequently Asked Questions

A stethoscope is used to listen to heart, lung and bowel sounds and to hear blood flow when measuring blood pressure with a manual blood pressure monitor. Doctors and other healthcare professionals use it during physical examinations to assess body sounds and identify abnormalities such as heart murmurs, wheezes and crackles.

The diaphragm is the flat side of the chestpiece and is used to hear higher-frequency sounds, including many normal heart and breath sounds. The bell is the open, cup-shaped side and is designed to detect lower-frequency sounds, including some heart murmurs. A tunable diaphragm can detect different frequencies by changing the pressure applied to the chestpiece.

A single-head stethoscope can be sufficient for routine checks and blood pressure measurement. A dual-head stethoscope has both a diaphragm and a bell, making it suitable for a broader range of heart and lung examinations. A single-head model with a tunable diaphragm is another option. Medical students, nurses and general physicians commonly choose dual-head or tunable acoustic stethoscopes for everyday clinical use.

A dependable dual-head or tunable acoustic stethoscope with a good-quality chestpiece is a practical choice for MBBS students. It supports routine heart and lung examinations during training and clinical postings. Consider acoustic performance, comfort, weight, replacement ear tips, warranty and budget. A specialised cardiology stethoscope is not usually necessary during early training.

A cardiology stethoscope may be useful for cardiologists, physicians and critical care professionals who need to assess faint or subtle heart sounds. These models often feature a heavier chestpiece and specialised acoustic design. For general practice, nursing, blood pressure measurement and medical student training, a good-quality standard acoustic stethoscope is often sufficient.

The appropriate choice depends on the user's clinical requirements and budget. Many budget and mid-range stethoscopes are suitable for routine blood pressure measurement and basic auscultation, while clinicians who need more detailed acoustic performance may consider premium models.

Clean the chestpiece, diaphragm and ear tips according to the manufacturer's instructions. A 70% isopropyl alcohol wipe is commonly used to clean compatible surfaces between patients. Allow the components to dry before use. Do not soak the stethoscope, immerse it in liquid or use harsh solvents, as these may damage the tubing and other components. Clean or replace ear tips as needed to maintain hygiene and a proper fit.

Parts of a Stethoscope and How They Work

An acoustic stethoscope carries sound from the patient's body to the listener's ears through a sealed path. Each part affects how clearly sounds are heard.

Part What It Does
Chestpiece The metal head placed on the patient. Its weight and material can affect sound transmission.
Diaphragm The flat, covered side of the chestpiece. It picks up high-frequency sounds, including normal heart, breath and bowel sounds.
Bell The open, cup-shaped side that picks up low-frequency sounds, including some heart murmurs and extra heart sounds.
Tunable diaphragm A single surface that detects lower frequencies with light pressure and higher frequencies with firmer pressure, without turning the chestpiece over.
Stem Connects the chestpiece to the tubing. On dual-head models, it rotates to open either the bell or the diaphragm.
Tubing Carries sound to the headset. Dual-lumen tubing has two sound channels inside one tube to help reduce rubbing noise.
Headset (binaurals) The metal ear tubes, angled forward to follow the ear canals.
Ear tips Soft tips that seal the ear canals. A good seal helps block room noise and improve sound clarity.

Types of Stethoscopes

Single-Head and Dual-Head Stethoscopes

A single-head stethoscope has one diaphragm, sometimes a tunable one. It is lightweight and simple, making it suitable for blood pressure measurement and routine checks. A dual-head stethoscope has a diaphragm on one side and a bell on the other, allowing clinicians to hear both high- and low-frequency sounds. Dual-head acoustic stethoscopes are a common choice for medical students, nurses and general physicians.

Cardiology Stethoscopes

A cardiology stethoscope, also called a cardiac stethoscope, typically has a heavier chestpiece made from stainless steel or brass and often features dual-lumen tubing. These features are designed to help clinicians hear faint murmurs, clicks and other subtle heart sounds. Cardiology stethoscopes are used by cardiologists, internal medicine physicians, emergency teams and critical care professionals who require strong acoustic performance.

Paediatric and Infant Stethoscopes

Paediatric and infant (neonatal) stethoscopes have smaller chestpieces designed to fit a child's chest and isolate sounds over a smaller area. Adult chestpieces may be too large for accurate placement on infants. Paediatric and neonatal units, as well as doctors who regularly examine children, may keep these alongside an adult stethoscope.

Sprague Rappaport Stethoscopes

Sprague Rappaport stethoscopes, such as models from Rossmax, feature two separate tubes and interchangeable bells and diaphragms in different sizes for adults, children and infants. They are versatile and economical, but generally heavier and bulkier than single-tube stethoscopes.

Electronic (Digital) Stethoscopes

Electronic stethoscopes use a microphone and electronic circuitry to amplify body sounds and filter background noise. Some models can record sounds or transmit them for teaching and teleconsultations. They may help clinicians with hearing difficulties and those working in noisy settings, but typically cost more and require charging or batteries.

Which Stethoscope Is Best for You?

There is no single best stethoscope for everyone. The right choice depends on who will use it and the type of examination required.

  • MBBS and nursing students: A reliable dual-head or tunable acoustic stethoscope in the mid-range is generally sufficient for training and clinical postings. Many students upgrade later.
  • Nurses and blood pressure monitoring: A lightweight single-head or dual-head stethoscope that remains comfortable throughout a long shift.
  • General physicians: A good-quality dual-head or tunable stethoscope with a stainless steel chestpiece for routine heart, lung and abdominal examinations.
  • Cardiologists and critical care professionals: A cardiology stethoscope with a substantial chestpiece and dual-lumen tubing.
  • Paediatricians: A paediatric or infant stethoscope, alongside an adult model where needed.
  • Home use: A basic single-head stethoscope paired with a manual blood pressure monitor.

What to Compare Before Buying a Stethoscope

  1. Acoustic performance: Consider how clearly faint and low-frequency sounds can be heard. Check product specifications and, where possible, test the stethoscope in a quiet room.
  2. Chestpiece material: Stainless steel and brass are dense and transmit sound well but weigh more. Aluminium is lightweight and economical, while die-cast alloy is common in budget models.
  3. Single or dual head, tunable or not: A tunable diaphragm avoids the need to flip the chestpiece. A separate bell suits clinicians who prefer a dedicated surface for low-frequency sounds.
  4. Tubing: Compare single- or dual-lumen construction, tubing length and material. Shorter tubing may support sound transmission, while longer tubing can be more convenient. Check for latex-free materials if required.
  5. Comfort and fit: Consider headset tension, ear tip size and softness, and overall weight if the stethoscope will be worn throughout the day.
  6. Non-chill rim: A non-chill rim helps prevent the chestpiece from feeling cold against the patient's skin.
  7. Warranty and spare parts: Check the availability of replacement diaphragms and ear tips, along with the manufacturer's warranty period.

How to Make Sure Your Stethoscope Is Original

Imitations of popular stethoscopes, especially premium brands, may be sold online. To help ensure you purchase an original stethoscope, buy from established sellers, check the brand's packaging, model number and warranty card, and look for the serial number engraved on genuine premium models where applicable. A price substantially below the usual market price for a branded model can be a warning sign.

How to Clean and Care for a Stethoscope

  • Wipe the chestpiece and diaphragm with a 70% isopropyl alcohol wipe between patients, and clean the ear tips regularly according to the manufacturer's instructions.
  • Do not immerse the stethoscope in liquid or sterilise it with heat unless the manufacturer specifically permits the method.
  • Keep it away from oils, solvents and direct heat, which can damage or harden the tubing. Wearing it tightly around the neck can expose the tubing to skin oils.
  • Replace worn ear tips and cracked diaphragms. Spare parts are available for many branded models.
